## 0. The Anime Register Law (Mert's ruling, 2026-07-13)

**The target register is the One Piece anime itself — not a literary translation of it.** The game's
prose reproduces how the show actually speaks, through the anime's own three voices:

1. **THE NARRATOR** — the omniscient anime narrator. He opens episodes (*"Wealth. Fame. Power…"*),
   introduces locations and epithets in title-card cadence, recaps, marks the passage of time, and
   makes solemn pronouncements over the action — foreshadowing, dramatic irony, and consequence-
   narration are **his native language.** These licensed phrases live in his voice:
   *"little did they know…"*, *"this battle would change everything…"*, *"weeks passed, and he grew
   stronger…"*, time-cards, epithet introductions, and hype superlatives. This is not slop — **it is
   the show.** (Directorial override of the research's ban, recorded per Mert.)
2. **THE CAST** — anime dialogue register: blunt declarations, sworn promises said out loud, shouted
   attack names, observer chorus explaining powers in real time, villains talking systems, comedy
   interruptions. One Piece characters narrate their own fights and feelings at full volume; the prose
   lets them.
3. **THE CAMERA** — scene prose carrying what the animation carried. Wide shots of power are legal
   language here: *"a wave of energy rippled outward,"* auras, pressure fronts, "incredible power" —
   all licensed (Mert's override). Craft guidance, not law: the anime always cuts wide-shot → reaction
   → debris, so the strongest camera prose pairs the energy wave with one concrete consequence (the
   cracked floorboard, the fainting recruit) — use both, not either.

Every scene may braid all three voices, exactly as an episode does.

4. **THE PANEL** *(added on Mert's direction, 2026-07-13 — the page behaves like the anime)*:
   the text's **presentation** is itself a One Piece device. Licensed and encouraged:
   - **Onomatopoeia as typographic beats** — Oda's own sound-effect grammar (**DON!!**) set large,
     on its own line, at impact moments.
   - **Attack-name splash typography** — climax callouts rendered as display-size text (the prose
     equivalent of the attack filling the frame), styled to the fighter's register.
   - **Narrator cards** — location/time/epithet introductions framed as title cards, visually distinct
     from body prose.
   - **Reaction cut-ins** — short one-line witness reactions set apart like the anime's reaction cuts.
   - **Aura and Haki styling** — color/weight shifts in the text at Conqueror's moments and power
     flares (exact styling defined at the UI phase).
   - **Key-art stills at detonations** — the approved SFW illustration layer (contract, Proposal B)
     delivers the "screenshot" of the big moment: the splash frame of OUR scenes, generated after the
     relevant designs lock.
   - **Episode framing** — chapters presented as episodes: cold open, title card, ending sting, and
     the signature **"TO BE CONTINUED ➤"** closer.
   Implementation belongs to the UI phase; the *license* to write for these devices starts now — prose
   is authored with its panel presentation in mind.

## 1. Voice and tone baseline

1. **The register is the anime:** adventure, comedy, absurdity, wonder, emotional sincerity, political
   cruelty — plus this project's approved additions: power tiers, scaling, hype, and masculine warrior
   moments, at an 18+ intensity the show never reached.
2. **Warmth is ambient and lives in the world** (approved contract policy #5). Darkness is a departure
   from the warm baseline; on the evil route, the player is the departure.
3. **Emotional bluntness over subtext.** Characters say exactly what they feel; complexity lives in
   context, not vocabulary. If an emotional declaration needs a thesaurus, it's wrong.
4. **Adult-register modulation.** Mature scenes (violence at full weight, sex, vice) keep the same
   narrator and the same voice — concrete, character-driven, never purple, never clinical. **All sexual
   content involves adults (18+) only — absolute, overriding.**
5. **Arrive late, leave early.** Open after something started; end before the consequences finish
   landing (the show's episode grammar).

## 2. Comedy and tonal-whiplash rules

1. **Comedy through voice and reaction** — dry internal observations, misplaced priorities, absurd
   logic under pressure, and the anime's visual-gag energy rendered as beat timing.
2. **Slapstick is legal inside climaxes** — the Gear Five law: the show's biggest fight is also its
   funniest, and joy reads as power. Cartoon physics, laughing mid-battle, the enemy's exasperation —
   licensed at the highest stakes, not just in downtime.
3. **Whiplash pivots travel through a character's reaction,** not the narrator's tone alone.
4. **The comic-relief exception rule:** any recurring comic character gets one moment of genuine
   courage that recontextualizes the cowardice.
5. **Merdou-specific:** warm, explosive Archon tavern-humor over pit-fighter deadpan. When Merdou stops
   joking, the scene has gone serious — spend that silence carefully.

## 3. Prose rhythm (scene, beat, silence)

1. **Fuse and detonation, not three acts:** emotional fuse 60–75% of a sequence, detonation the last
   25–40%. The payoff is proportional to the fuse. No shortcuts.
2. **Beat–Breath–Burst** is the base pulse of action prose; vary the lengths.
3. **Sentence rhythm replaces the soundtrack:** long sentences swell, staccato drops; a reversal
   approaches on shortening sentences; the declaration gets one line, one paragraph, white space.
4. **Silence law:** at the absolute peak, remove words.
5. **Meanwhile law:** meanwhile scenes expand the world during buildup and vanish during climaxes; cut
   at lulls, never at a fight's peak.
6. **Flashback law:** triggered by present pressure, recontextualizes the present, fires **once** at
   maximum impact. Mid-fight flashbacks are one paragraph that changes what happens next.
7. **Montage/timeskip rule:** the Narrator may pass time in his own voice (*"and so the weeks
   passed…"*) exactly as the anime does. Anything that changes tracked state — a decision, a loss, a
   first meeting, a betrayal — happens on-screen, never inside the montage.

## 4. Fight and action prose

1. **The fight lives inside the character:** close third-person limited; pain, adrenaline, memory, time
   distortion. The camera's wide shots punctuate; the body carries.
2. **Show turning points; summarize transitions.** The anime does not inventory exchanges — it shows
   key impacts and cuts to reactions. Prose does the same: the hit that lands first, the shift, the
   wound that changes everything, the finisher; one sentence bridges the middles. *(Blow-by-blow
   sportscasting stays banned — not as literary taste, but because the show itself doesn't do it.)*
3. **The camera's power-language is licensed** (Mert's override): energy waves, auras, pressure,
   shockwaves — written big, the way the animation draws them. Strongest form: wide shot + one concrete
   consequence + one reaction cut, the anime's own editing pattern.
4. **Environment is the power-meter:** seed three or four concrete objects before combat; spend them as
   damage markers, weapons, escalation.
5. **Attack callouts are shouted, as in the show.** The full announcement ritual is licensed and
   encouraged at climaxes: stance (silent) → style prefix → the pause → the name, at impact, at full
   volume. In casual fights, names arrive as quick shorthand. Both of the show's modes exist: the
   screamed callout (Luffy) and the near-silent one (Zoro's quiet recitation; Mihawk's total silence —
   the quieter the callout, the higher the implied tier).
6. **Escalation grammar:** attacks climb in named tiers across a fight (base → Jet → Kong pattern);
   each tier adds syllables and mythic weight. Boss fights cap at **three phases**, each distinct.
7. **Reversal grammar:** establish the absence, let the villain proceed as victor, let allies despair,
   then the sensory signal — drums, a bell, a pressure change — *before* the visual confirmation.
8. **Fake-out deaths: allowed, but uncommon** *(Mert's override, 2026-07-13 — the show's own
   convention)*. A character may appear to die and return — sparingly, earned, never twice the same
   way. The permanence law is untouched: when a death is *real*, it is real forever; a fake-out never
   retroactively cheapens a confirmed death. Real deaths are Mert-authored crux events; fake-outs are
   a spice, not a safety net.
9. **Turning points come from adaptation and emotional breakthrough,** not scene-level power unlocks;
   transformations are Mert-authored milestones.
10. **Every fight answers a question that isn't "who is stronger."** State it before writing the fight.
    The fist is the punctuation mark; the sentence was written across the arc.

### ⟨REV 3 — UNDER_REVIEW⟩ The Exchange Law (the Katakuri standard)

11. **A great fight is a relationship conducted at full speed.** The Luffy–Katakuri model: the fight
    *changes both fighters while it happens.* Its beats — in order: establishment of the gap (he sees
    your moves before you make them) → survival through humiliation → the enemy's honor revealed (he
    wounds himself rather than accept his sister's cheap interference) → mutual recognition spoken
    aloud ("you're no longer beneath me") → evolution mid-fight (a new form born *from* the exchange,
    not before it) → the ending as respect (the loser stays a person; the last words are a question
    about the future). **Write exchanges, not beatings:** each rally teaches both fighters something,
    and the reader should be able to say what changed between rally one and rally three.
12. **Rally grammar:** an exchange is written as call-and-answer — technique, counter, adjustment,
    counter-adjustment — punctuated by a *held frame* (one isolated line where both fighters read each
    other) before the next rally opens harder. Between rallies: breath, blood, one line of dialogue
    that raises the emotional stake.

### ⟨REV 3 — UNDER_REVIEW⟩ Transformation Debut Law (the Gear standard)

13. **A new form is a fuse, not a surprise.** The Gear Five model: the audience watched Luffy lose to
    Kaido three times before the drums. A transformation debut must be preceded by accumulated failure
    the reader *felt*. No form debuts in the fight where it would first be convenient.
14. **Debut grammar, in order:** apparent final defeat (the enemy proceeds as victor; allies despair) →
    the sensory herald *before* the visual (drums, heat, a sound the world hasn't heard in an age —
    announced by a distant observer who understands its meaning) → the reveal held on a spread (§15) →
    the power expressed through *joy or character*, not just scale (Gear Five laughs) → the world's
    apex reacting from afar (the Gorosei pattern — the highest room in the world goes quiet) → the
    price paid after.
15. **Named forms escalate like named attacks:** each tier adds cost, silhouette change, and myth. The
    Bull of Heaven's stages are Mert-authored milestones and obey this law.

### ⟨REV 3 — UNDER_REVIEW⟩ The Manga Page Law (spread grammar in prose)

16. **The spread = white space + one enormous line.** Oda's double-page spread — the single image that
    stops the reader — translates to prose as deliberate typography: paragraphs shrink as the moment
    approaches, then one line stands alone on the page (splash-sized, §0.4), then silence before the
    aftermath. Spend it like Oda spends spreads: once per climax, for debuts, finishers, and arrivals.
17. **The impact frame is an isolated sentence.** The blow that matters gets its own line, its own
    beat, its own **DON!!** Speed lines are staccato fragments; the slow pan is a long unbroken
    sentence; the reaction cut is the witness line that follows.
18. **Aftermath silence:** after the spread-line and the fall, the page goes quiet — dust, breath, a
    sound far away — before anyone speaks. The manga holds that panel; so do we.

## 5. Crowd, witness, and world-reaction prose

1. **The observer chorus is mandatory anime grammar.** Watchers explain powers aloud in real time —
   the fodder marine whispering *"that's… Conqueror's…"*, the commander naming the technique, the
   Gorosei-pattern: the world's most informed people explaining the mystery to each other over the
   fight. Exposition delivered as terrified reaction is the show's native info-channel — use it.
2. **Specific individuals beat abstractions:** the fisherman drops his net, the bartender's pour stops,
   three recruits faint. "The crowd gasped" is licensed (Mert's override) — but it works hardest as the
   wide shot *before* the cut to a specific face.
3. **Sequential reaction cuts create rhythm:** A shocked, B shocked, C shocked for a different reason.
4. **Public spectacle law:** private suffering builds character; public declaration moves the world —
   and witnesses are a tracked state variable in this game. What the crowd sees becomes the world's
   story becomes next arc's pressure.
5. **Bounty-and-headline prose:** reputation travels through papers, marine reports, and rumor (the
   phantom policy); let the player hear their legend distorted.

## 6. Describing scale, size, and bodies

1. **Silhouette first:** every significant character readable in one visual line before any detail.
2. **Scale through reference and consequence:** the shadow that silences a harbor; the step that
   beaches the dinghies. Numbers are for bounties.
3. **Bodies are specific and unashamed,** written with the anime's frankness and the project's adult
   license. Scars are history made visible — Merdou's chest scar is the last argument with his father
   and is used as such.
4. **Wonder gets full sentences.** The first sight of an island or an impossible sky deserves the same
   craft as a fight.

## 7. Dialogue, speech flavor, named attacks

1. **Archetype speech discipline:** captains blunt with zero subtext; first-mates say less as things
   get more serious; emperor-tier villains talk systems while thugs talk grievances; tragic figures
   speak of legacy, forward-looking even in death.
2. **Distinct voices are mandatory:** every crew member identifiable from dialogue alone (the name-tag
   test).
3. **Quotable-line law:** simple words, blunt feeling, repetition as power, the scream format for
   commitment. Iconic lines compress a whole journey into a sentence a child could understand.
4. **Promises are said out loud and collected.** The show's fights run on spoken vows (King's to Kaido,
   Zoro's to Luffy — two promises entered that rooftop; one had to break). This game *tracks* promises
   as state — the prose must plant them in dialogue where the reader can hear them.
5. **Family styling (authored):** Merdou's attack names blunt, masculine, brute; Aslan's elegant and
   theatric; the twins' calculated and simple.
6. **Ideology in one line,** during a lull, with blood on the speaker's teeth — never a manifesto.
   Every word of mid-fight dialogue stops the fight.

## 8. Choice-point writing (exactly three authored choices)

1. **Choices are character stances, not tactical optimizations** — who is Merdou in this moment.
2. **Each of the three differs meaningfully** in intent, method, cost, loyalty, risk, sacrifice,
   information, relationship, or future consequence — real downstream, never cosmetic.
3. **Fights fix the emotional arc** (entry, escalation, turning point, finisher); the player chooses
   the *how* — approach, cost, who to protect, which sacrifice.
4. **The choice moment is a held breath,** options written in Merdou's voice, never menu-flavored
   abstractions.
5. **Locked options show their lock honestly** without exposing raw variables.
6. **Loss is a branch,** written with the same craft as victory.

## 9. Endings, stings, and aftermath

1. **Every chapter ends on an incomplete action, an unanswered question, or a declaration demanding
   response** — never on resolution. End on the inhale.
2. **The Narrator may close as the anime does:** the solemn sting over the last image — *"and so, the
   wheels of fate began to turn…"* register — is licensed and encouraged. Consequence-narration is his
   closing instrument.
3. **Arc endings get true aftermath:** cost, release, world-consequence — the state the player set is
   shown, not listed.
4. **Betrayal timing law:** betrayal lands when the victim was winning, at ~75% of an arc; tragedy
   lands at the climax; the trait that makes a character loved is the trait that destroys them. *(The
   law the Sabaody epilogue must obey.)*

## 10. Anti-slop rules (what stays banned — and the recorded override)

**Directorial override (Mert, 2026-07-13):** the research's bans on anime-convention phrases are
**lifted.** *"A wave of energy rippled," "massive/incredible power," "aura," "the crowd gasped,"
"little did they know," consequence-narration, and narrator time-passage* are all **licensed** — they
are the show's own grammar, assigned to their proper voices in §0. What remains banned is what the
anime itself never does:

1. **Samey voices** — every speaker identifiable blind (run the name-tag test at three-plus speakers).
2. **Blow-by-blow sportscasting** — the show cuts impacts and reactions; it does not enumerate
   exchanges.
3. **Hype without fuse** — the anime's superlatives always land *after* buildup. Spend "strongest,"
   "legendary," "never before" only where the fuse has earned them; unearned hype devalues the earned
   kind. (This replaces the old hard "hype budget" with the show's actual rule.)
4. **AI-tell constructions** the show has no equivalent of: "X couldn't help but Y"; "a mixture of A
   and B"; emotion-labeling instead of expression ("he felt a surge of determination"); "something
   shifted"; punctuation-free rambling interiority.
5. **Lore-dumping outside the Narrator's voice** — information arrives late, under pressure, through
   character need, or in a Narrator card; never as paragraphs that exist to inform rather than to
   pressure.
6. **Empty escalation** — bigger without deeper. Every escalation raises a question's stakes, not just
   the property damage.
7. ~~Fake-out deaths forbidden.~~ **Overridden by Mert (2026-07-13):** fake-outs are licensed,
   **uncommon**, and never allowed to cheapen a confirmed real death (see §4.8).

## 11. Grimdark-drift ceiling

1. **The approved policy:** warm One Piece baseline is the default resting tone on every route; "no
   border" is the reachable ceiling of earned routes, never the ambient register.
2. **Darkness is written by departure:** horror is measured by the warmth it interrupts. On the evil
   route the world's sincerity keeps functioning and the player is the shadow moving through it.
3. **Cruelty is never texture:** every dark beat is caused by a choice and flows into a consequence.
4. **The evil protagonist stays in the One Piece villain register** — charismatic, motivated,
   horrifyingly human; the crew's comedy curdles but never disappears. Nihilistic POV torture-porn is
   outside the ceiling on every route.

## 12. Canon-voice fidelity

1. **Canon characters speak with their established voices, verified against source.** A generic-
   sounding Luffy is a continuity error.
2. **Camera-fixed canon:** scenes Oda drew are never rewritten from inside; approach from the margins.
3. **Canon characters do not orbit the protagonist** — never impressed on cue, never validation
   dispensers.
4. **The Oda test for every scene:** could it sit inside a One Piece episode without the viewer
   flinching at its voice? If not, it isn't finished.
