## 1. Tools (use when they serve the scene)

- **Visible objective.** Someone on screen wants something concrete within the scene (finish the
  drink, collect the debt, get the girl's name). If no one wants anything visible, the scene isn't
  ready.
- **Physical staging.** Establish the space in one or two concrete lines (what the room is, where
  the exits and levels are). Track who stands where; movement means something.
- **Entrances.** A character enters doing something characteristic, not being described.
- **Interruption.** Let events, arrivals, and other speakers cut lines off. Completed speeches are
  rare; interrupted ones are the norm.
- **Props.** Give scenes two or three objects that get used (the mug, the ledger, the stew pot).
  A prop doing work beats a paragraph describing mood.
- **Reactions.** After a significant beat, show one or two **specific, named or visually distinct**
  witnesses reacting in ways particular to them — when witnesses are present and their reaction
  advances the scene. (Not a quota. Empty rooms are allowed. See §2.)
- **Background activity.** Ordinary life continues behind the plot (the accordion keeps playing).
  One background beat per scene is usually enough.
- **Dialogue economy.** Characters say what they want and feel bluntly, in their own registers
  (name-tag test: identifiable with tags removed). Cut any line that exists to sound quotable.
- **Public consequences.** What witnesses see becomes reputation, rumor, and tracked state
  (`STORY_ENGINE.md`). If an act should echo, stage it where the world can see it — a design
  choice, made per scene.
- **Emotional declarations.** Delivered by characters, out loud, in simple words — never narrated
  as feelings ("he felt…") and never explained after being shown.
- **Comedy from behavior.** Jokes come from what characters do and want (misplaced priorities,
  absurd logic, physical failure) — not from narrator wit. If a joke needs the narrator to land,
  give it to a character or cut it.
- **Scene cuts and reveals.** Enter late, leave on movement. A reveal is an image or a line the
  scene has earned — place it at a cut, don't caption it.
- **The narrator (sparse).** Opening card (facts: place, time), at most one mid-scene transition,
  one closing line. Formal, grave, brief — a voice of public history (cards, headlines, time
  passage, consequence). Budget guideline: ≤10% of scene words. He never jokes, interprets
  feelings, explains themes, or announces importance. He may surface world-state diegetically
  (headlines, bounties, rumor).

## 2. Explicitly rejected (former "laws" now void)

The following are **not rules of this project**, whatever any archived document says:

- Mandatory witness reactions or a fixed reaction-chain at every climax.
- Mandatory comedy in every fight (or any per-scene humor quota).
- Mandatory silence after every finisher; silence-as-proof-of-power.
- Mandatory aphorisms, stings, or "quotable line" engineering.
- Markdown/emoji typography pretending to be manga panels; onomatopoeia as headings.
  (Presentation effects are UI-phase render decisions, not prose.)
- Aura prose: menace or power asserted by the narrator ("something enormous…") instead of staged
  through action and specific witness behavior.
- Universal arc formulas (invariant four-stage engines, fixed fuse/detonation percentages, fixed
  betrayal timing, "never end on resolution").
- Narrator explanation of themes, jokes, psychology, or the meaning of a choice.
- "Oda paint" — genre decoration (epithets, DON, callouts, hype superlatives) used as a substitute
  for actual staging. Anime devices are legal *when a specific scene calls for them*; they earn
  nothing by mere presence.
- Mandatory interiority. Internal monologue is off by default; a scene may use it only with a
  per-scene note approved by Mert.
